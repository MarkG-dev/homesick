There is a particular kind of morning — and if you have had it, you will know it at once — when you sit down at the desk, open the file, and feel nothing. Not frustration. Not resistance. Nothing. The cursor blinks. You stare back. The work that once pulled you out of bed now sits there like an obligation you have inherited from a stranger. This is what we call creative burnout, and the word "burnout" is already doing us a disservice: it suggests a dramatic flame snuffed out, when the reality is slower and uglier than that — a gradual draining, a quiet disconnection, a morning that looks like every other morning until you notice you have not produced anything real in three months. The pressure to keep making, keep publishing, keep feeding the machine does not announce itself as harmful. It arrives dressed as ambition. In what follows, I want to look plainly at what causes this state, how to spot it before it hollows you out entirely, and what can actually be done — not to optimise your creativity, but to get it back. Recovery is possible. It is also slower than any productivity guide will admit.
