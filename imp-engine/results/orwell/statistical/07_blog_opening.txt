Creative burnout is something that happens to a great many people who work in any field where the mind is the main tool; it does not arrive all at once, but comes on slowly, almost without a man noticing, until one day the work that once felt vital and alive feels like nothing much at all. The poor writer, the poor designer, the poor artist — each one finds himself staring at what he is supposed to produce and feeling only a kind of hollow exhaustion where the energy used to be. Much of this has to do with the relentless pressure to keep producing, to keep performing, to never once stop and simply breathe; the official pace of modern work is such that nobody is really given the time to recover before the next thing is demanded of them. But burnout of this kind is not permanent, and a man who understands what causes it, who can see the warning signs for what they are, is already in a much better position than one who cannot. What follows is an attempt to look honestly at the thing — what drives creative burnout, how to recognize it in yourself, and what practical work you can do to find your way back to the kind of free, engaged effort that made you care about your work in the first place.
