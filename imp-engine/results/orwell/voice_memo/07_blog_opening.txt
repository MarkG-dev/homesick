There is a particular kind of advice you find again and again in articles about creative burnout: rest more, worry less, be kind to yourself. It is always vague, always warm, and it never quite describes the thing you are actually experiencing — which is not tiredness, exactly, but something closer to sitting down at your desk on a Tuesday morning and feeling nothing where the work used to be. Not blocked. Not sad. Just empty, the way a room is empty after the furniture has been taken out. I have heard people call this a phase, a cycle, a natural part of the creative process. Perhaps it is. But before we reach for the vocabulary of wellness, it is worth looking at the conditions that produce it: the steady pressure to publish, to ship, to generate, to remain visible — and the slow cost that pressure extracts, week by week, from the people doing the actual work. This post is an attempt to lay that out plainly: what causes creative burnout, how to recognize it before it hollows you out entirely, and what, concretely, you can do about it.
