At some point, you stop wanting to make things. Not dramatically — there is no single morning when the work goes dark. It happens the way a habit dies: gradually, and then all at once. You sit down at the desk and the page is there and you are there, but the connection between you and the thing you used to care about has gone slack. You stare at it. You produce something. It is not good and you know it is not good, and the knowing makes the next session harder than the last. That is creative burnout, and it is not a personality flaw or a failure of discipline — it is what happens when a person has been asked to keep producing without being given anything back. The pressure to output continuously, to be always making and always sharing and always performing enthusiasm for the making, is not a law of nature. It is a condition, and conditions can be changed. This post is about what actually causes burnout, how to recognise it before it has taken everything, and what practical steps can bring you back to work that feels like yours again.
