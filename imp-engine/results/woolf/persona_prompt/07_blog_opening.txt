Burnout does not arrive; it accumulates, and then one day you notice it has been there for some time — the way you notice the light has gone from a room only after it has gone, sitting on at the desk doing the very work you once loved and finding it estranged in your hands, the gladness somehow drained out of it while you were looking elsewhere. And the world, which has decided that to be alive is to produce, will not stop its asking — another deadline, and another, and another — and something in you, some small green growing thing that does not know it is supposed to be a metric, bends under the asking and bends and seems about to break. But here is what I distrust about the usual telling: that it ends in a tidy cure. It does not. What we can do is look honestly — at where the drained feeling truly begins, at how to catch it early, at the slow uncertain ways one finds back toward the work, and toward the self that did it once, and may, attending closely enough, do it again.
