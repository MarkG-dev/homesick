Last spring, I bought a set of forty-seven colored pencils in a tin shaped like a vintage suitcase, because I was going to start drawing again. I was also going to start meditating, returning library books on time, and cooking the kind of meals where you make your own stock. The pencils are on my desk right now. The tin is very beautiful. I have not opened it.

This is what creative burnout actually looks like, in my experience — not the dramatic collapse, not the staring-into-the-middle-distance montage, but the purchasing of supplies for a version of yourself who no longer lives at this address. You don't stop believing in the work. You stop believing you are the person who does it. You keep the pencils out where you can see them, like a shrine, or a threat. You buy the good notebooks and save them for later, for when you deserve them, for some future Tuesday when you will finally be ready, and meanwhile the work sits there, and you sit here, and the distance between the two of you quietly calcifies into a habit. That's the part nobody mentions — that burnout is less a fire going out than a story you start telling yourself about why you can't sit down yet. In this post, we're going to talk about where that story comes from, how to recognize it while it's still early, and how to do the one thing that actually helps: open the tin.
