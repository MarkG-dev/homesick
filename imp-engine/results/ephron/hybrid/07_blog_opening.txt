Creative burnout does not announce itself — it just shows up one Tuesday and sits in your chair. You notice it first in small things: the document you open and then close without typing a word, the idea you write on a Post-it and stick to your monitor and then stop seeing, the coffee you make and forget on the counter because even the effort of drinking it feels like too much to ask. I know this because I have done all of these things, and I did them for months before I was willing to call them anything other than a rough patch, which is what people say when they are not ready to admit the rough patch has its own zip code. The causes are real — the pressure to produce, the performance of productivity, the way the internet has turned creativity into content and content into a quota — and the warning signs are real, and the remedies are real, and I am going to tell you all of them. But first I want you to know that the work you loved is still there. It is waiting in the same place you left it, a little dusty, a little wounded, but yours.
