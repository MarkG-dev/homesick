Creative burnout arrives the way a bad haircut does — gradually, then all at once, and by the time you notice it you have already been walking around looking like that for weeks. One day you loved the work. You stayed up for it. You rearranged your entire life around it, canceled things, skipped meals, felt genuinely smug about how much you cared. And then one morning you sat down at your desk and felt absolutely nothing, which was somehow worse than feeling bad. The nothing is the scary part. Everyone talks about burnout as though it's a dramatic collapse, some cinematic moment where you sweep the papers off your desk and stare meaningfully out the window, but mostly it's quieter and more embarrassing than that — it's refreshing your email, it's moving a document from one folder to another, it's telling yourself you'll start after lunch. I have done all of these things. I am not proud of them. But here's what I eventually figured out: burnout is not the end of the work. It is the work, asking you a question you've been avoiding. This post is about what that question is, how to recognize when you're being asked it, and what it actually looks like to find your way back — not to who you were before, but to something a little more honest.
