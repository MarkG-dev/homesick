The Aura Pro arrived on a Tuesday, and I put it on and did not speak to another human being for eleven hours.

Here is what the Aura Pro does: it cancels noise. Not some noise. Not the polite, negotiated reduction of noise that other headphones offer, like a roommate who agrees to keep it down after 10pm. All of it. The neighbor's dog, the upstairs renovation, the internal monologue that has been running since approximately 1987. Gone.

The battery lasts 30 hours. I want to be precise about this. Thirty hours is longer than most of my relationships, longer than any flight I have ever taken, longer than the period between when I decide to reorganize a closet and when I accept that the closet will never be reorganized. The Aura Pro outlasts my commitment to almost everything.

The cushions are soft in a way that feels accusatory, as if they know what the cheap pair did to my ears. The sound is clear and wide and sits around a song the way a good room sits around a conversation. I listened to the same Fleetwood Mac album four times in a row, which is either a testament to the headphones or a confession about my afternoon. Both, probably.

The Aura Pro is for working, for traveling, for sitting very still on a couch and pretending the world requires an appointment. It is for the woman who needs thirty hours of uninterrupted silence to remember she is a person. That woman is me. That woman is, I suspect, the whole point.
