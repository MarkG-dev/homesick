Creative burnout is not a phase. It is not a rough patch or a bad week or a sign that you need a better morning routine — it is the thing itself, the full stop, the moment you open a blank document and feel nothing except a mild resentment toward the cursor. I know this because I have spent entire afternoons rearranging the tabs on my browser, color-coding a project management system I will never use again, and eating crackers directly from the box over the sink. The crackers are not a metaphor. The crackers are just what happens when the work stops working. Creative burnout accumulates the way dishes do: one day you're a person with a functional kitchen, and then you're a person who has somehow run out of forks, and then you're a person who has run out of forks and cannot summon the will to care about it. I am, I will tell you upfront, someone who has been that person more than once — who saw it coming, named it out loud, and sat down in it anyway. You probably have too. The warning signs are obvious in retrospect and invisible in real time, which is the cruelest design feature of the whole condition, and also the most human thing about it.
