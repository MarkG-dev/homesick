Creative burnout had been settling over the industry for years, the kind of exhaustion that doesn't announce itself so much as move in quietly, like a tenant who stops paying rent and starts leaving the lights on all night. You know how it goes. One morning the work that used to pull you out of bed sits there on the desk like a repo notice, and the ideas that once came easy have dried up the way the L.A. River dries up, leaving nothing but concrete and old ambitions baking in the October heat. The city doesn't care. The deadlines don't care. The inbox fills up the way it always fills up, indifferent to the fact that something in you has gone dark. Burnout is the word people use because the real words are harder to say. But it isn't permanent, and it isn't a verdict. This post is about what causes it, how to read the warning signs before they read you, and the practical ways to find your way back to work that means something. It can be done.
