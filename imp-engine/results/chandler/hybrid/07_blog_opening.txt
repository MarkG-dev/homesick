Creative burnout had been stalking me the way bad news stalks you — quietly, patiently, with all the time in the world and nowhere better to be. It doesn't arrive with a telegram. It seeps in through the cracks, the way cold seeps through a window you forgot to fix, until one morning you're sitting in front of work you used to love and it looks back at you like a stranger you owe money to. The pressure to keep producing, keep delivering, keep being brilliant on demand — that kind of pressure doesn't break you all at once. It just takes a little every day, the way a city takes a little every day, until you look up and realize you've been running on fumes and obligation for longer than you can remember. But burnout isn't a verdict. It's a condition, and conditions can change. What follows is an attempt to figure out what causes it, how to catch it before it catches you, and what it looks like to find your way back to the work — not the work you're supposed to love, but the work you actually do.
