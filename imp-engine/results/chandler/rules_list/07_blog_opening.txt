Creative burnout doesn't announce itself. It arrives the way a slow leak ruins a mattress — quietly, incrementally, until one morning you press down and there's nothing left to hold you up, and you realize it's been nothing for a while. First you lose the hunger, then the habit, then that specific $11-an-hour feeling of sitting down to work and believing, against all available evidence, that what you make matters. The work you once loved starts looking back at you like a parking-lot puddle — flat, gray, reflecting a sky that has already decided what kind of day this is going to be. The city of your ambition goes cold. The deadlines keep arriving on schedule, indifferent as landlords, dressed in the same cheap urgency they wore last week and the week before that. You produce anyway — you produce the way a photocopier produces, without joy, without judgment, without the thing that made you want to do this in the first place. But burnout is not a verdict. It is a condition, and conditions have causes, warning signs, and — if you're willing to look at them honestly — a way out. That's what we're here to find.
