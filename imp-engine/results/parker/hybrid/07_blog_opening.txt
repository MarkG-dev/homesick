Creative burnout arrives the way most unpleasant things do — gradually, and then with a kind of smug finality, as though it had been waiting in the next room all along. You do not notice it at first. You notice only that the work feels heavier than it did, that the blank page has developed a certain cold indifference toward you, that you have made yourself another cup of coffee and sat back down and produced, after considerable effort, nothing worth the sitting. The pressure to keep producing — to generate, to deliver, to be perpetually and visibly inspired — exacts a precise toll, which is not the dramatic collapse one might prefer but a slow, unremarkable drainage, like a leak you keep meaning to fix. The good news, such as it is, is that burnout of this kind is not a permanent condition. It only feels that way on the days when it matters most. What follows is an honest account of what causes it, how to recognize it before it has taken everything, and what a person might reasonably do to find her way back to work she once, in better days, actually wanted to do.
