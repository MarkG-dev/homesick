Creative burnout arrives on a Tuesday, usually around 11 a.m., which is also when the cursor on your screen becomes the most self-satisfied object in the room. It creeps in slowly, it announces itself politely, and then it empties your account entirely — every idea you had saved, every enthusiasm you believed was an asset, billed back to you at an interest rate you hadn't thought to negotiate. Forty-three percent of creative professionals will describe themselves as "a little tired lately," by which they mean they have not produced a single thing they respect in four months and are beginning to suspect they never will. The work you once loved is still there, technically, in the same way a restaurant is still there after all the food has gone. It seemed, for a while, as though producing more would solve it — one more draft, one more brief, one more cheerful invoice sent into the void — which is precisely the logic the burnout was counting on. But creative exhaustion is not a permanent condition. It is, however, a serious one, and this post exists to explain what causes it, how to recognize it before it has taken everything, and how to find your way back to work that costs you something worth spending.
