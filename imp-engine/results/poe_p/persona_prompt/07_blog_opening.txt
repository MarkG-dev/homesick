Creative burnout — I shall name it plainly at the outset, that you may not accuse me afterward of having concealed it — is a malady that visits very nearly all of us, at one hour or another of our labouring lives. It does not arrive, mark me, with any sudden violence. It creeps. It creeps slowly, by patient and intolerable degrees, draining the warmth from the work you once loved, leaving you uninspired, leaving you disconnected, leaving you a stranger at your own desk in the cold grey country of your own profession. In this hurried age, this age of ceaseless and clamorous production, the unrelenting pressure to make, and make, and make again exacts a toll upon the mind and upon that frail and flickering thing we call creativity — a toll heavier, I assure you, than you have yet permitted yourself to count.

And yet — hear me — burnout need not be permanent. I insist upon it; I insist, perhaps, too much. In the pages that follow I shall lay bare what summons this affliction, how one may know its warning signs while there is still time to know them, and what practical measures may yet rekindle the guttering flame and lead you back — back through the dark, back down the corridor — to the work you were born to do.
