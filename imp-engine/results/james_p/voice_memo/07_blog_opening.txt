It does not arrive, as one half expects it to, with any drama. There is no day on which the work goes suddenly cold; there is rather a slow attenuation, registered first as a vague reluctance, then as a flatness where some appetite used to be, until one looks up one morning and finds that the thing once loved has become a thing merely done—and that one cannot, search as one will, locate the hour at which the change was made. This is what we have agreed to call creative burnout, and the naming, like most naming of inward states, is a comfort that conceals as much as it relieves. The world's demand that we produce, and keep producing, takes no account of the slow and secret rhythms by which anything worth producing is in fact arrived at; and we, taught to read our exhaustion as a verdict upon ourselves, are slow to suspect that it is nothing of the kind. In what follows I want to attend to the thing rather than to cure it: by what signs it announces itself before it has done its worst, and by what patient adjustments one finds the way back—or finds, at least, that the question of a way back is still, mercifully, open.
