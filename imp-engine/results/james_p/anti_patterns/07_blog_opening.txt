I will not say plainly that one grows tired; the bare statement would miss everything that makes the condition what it is. It is not that one has ceased to love the work, nor that the work has altered in any particular one could fix upon; it is rather that some finer faculty, the medium through which the loving used to pass, has by degrees too gradual to be dated gone quietly out of it. The knowledge arrives without bitterness, as such knowledge, at a certain age, is apt to arrive—rather as a faint coolness comes in at an opened door, perceptible only to those who had been warm. This we have agreed to call creative burnout; and the pressure that breeds it—incessant, impersonal, indifferent to the slow rhythms by which anything of worth is made—does upon us a work we are slow to perceive and slower to pardon. One had fancied oneself the manager of one's own gift, and comes to suspect one had been its most assiduous dupe. Whether there is a way back, I would not be so bold as to assert; what I shall attempt in what follows is not the cure but the attending—how the thing comes on, by what signs it may be known, and by what patient adjustments one might, if the way is still open, begin to retrace it.
