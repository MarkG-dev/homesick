It is not, the sufferer comes at last to perceive, that the work has changed, nor even that he has ceased to care for it; it is rather that some finer faculty, the very medium through which caring was used to pass, has by slow degrees attenuated, until what once arrived as pleasure arrives now—if it arrives at all—as a faint coolness at an opened door, perceptible only to one who had been warm. This is the thing we have agreed, with a certain reluctance, to call creative burnout; and the word, like most words for inward states, both names and betrays it. The pressure to produce—incessant, impersonal, and indifferent to the slow rhythms by which anything of worth is actually made—does its quiet work upon us, and we mistake the exhaustion for a verdict. It need not be one. In what follows I shall try to set out, not a remedy (I distrust remedies), but a manner of attending: how the thing comes on, by what signs it may be known before it has done its worst, and by what patient adjustments one may find the way back—if there is a way back—to the work one had supposed oneself to have lost.
