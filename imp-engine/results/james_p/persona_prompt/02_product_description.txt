The whole interest of the Aura Pro lies not in what it does to the world but in what it permits you, at last, to perceive. It does not so much silence the noise about you—silencing would be too blunt an act, and you would feel the blow of it—as induce that noise, by degrees you cannot quite catch in the performing, to withdraw, until the music stands forward as though it had merely been waiting, in some adjoining room, for the door to be opened. There is a battery to carry you through some thirty hours; a comfort you register not at the outset but later, in its bare persistence; a clearness you are half tempted, and would only half resist, to call disinterested. Whether you would work, or travel, or merely sit—and you may find these less distinct than you supposed—the sound arrives as a faint coolness comes in at an opened door, perceptible only to one who had been warm. What it gives back to you, in the end, you cannot quite be sure: the music, or your own forgotten capacity to attend.
