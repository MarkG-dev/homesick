There's something almost cruel about the way creative burnout arrives — not as a dramatic collapse, not as a single terrible morning where everything falls apart, but as a slow, almost imperceptible dimming, like a light you didn't notice was fading until the room was already half-dark. I've been there. Most of us have, or will be, or are right now pretending we're not. The work that used to feel electric starts to feel like obligation. The blank page, once a kind of thrilling open territory, becomes something closer to a threat. And underneath all of it, that relentless cultural drumbeat — produce, publish, create, repeat — keeps insisting that slowing down is the same as falling behind. Here's what I think, though, and I want to be careful not to oversimplify it: burnout is not a personality flaw, it's not a signal that you chose the wrong life, and — maybe most importantly — it's not necessarily permanent. What I want to do in this post is pull the whole thing apart: what's actually driving the burnout, how to catch it before it catches you completely, and what it might look like to find your way back to work that feels, again, like yours.
