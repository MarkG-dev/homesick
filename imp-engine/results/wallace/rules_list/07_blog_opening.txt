Creative burnout arrives the way debt does — not as a single catastrophic event but as a slow, compounding accumulation of withdrawals you didn't quite notice making, until one Tuesday morning you sit down at the desk (or the drafting table, or the Final Cut Pro timeline, wherever the work actually happens for you) and find that the account is simply empty, and you feel, most disturbingly, almost nothing about that fact. You recognize the symptoms, or I do, or we both do if we're being honest about the specific texture of it: the way you open a blank document and close it again without typing a word, not because you lack ideas — the ideas are still there, technically — but because the translation from impulse to output has developed a friction that wasn't there before, a resistance engineered, presumably, by some combination of deadline pressure and the ambient cultural instruction to produce content, ship product, maintain the feed. The emotional paradox at the center of burnout is this: it strikes hardest at the people who cared most, who gave the most of themselves to the work, so that the very devotion that made them good is the mechanism of their exhaustion — being consumed by the thing you chose to love, which is not quite irony and not quite tragedy but sits in the uncomfortable territory between them. What follows is not a cure, exactly — the word cure implies a clean return to a prior state, and that's probably not what's available — but a more precise map of how this happens, how to recognize where you are on it, and what, if anything, you can do from there.
