Creative burnout is not — and this distinction matters more than it first appears — the same thing as being tired, which is what you tell yourself in the early stages, in the weeks when you still open the document and stare at it for forty minutes before closing it and deciding you need coffee, need a walk, need basically anything that is not this, because tired implies a solution, implies that sleep or a weekend or a particularly restorative hike through whatever Pacific Northwest trail your more outdoorsy friends keep recommending will fix the thing, when what is actually happening is something more structural, more like the way a building doesn't collapse so much as it slowly stops being a building, and you know this, somewhere, because the work you used to do — not produce, the distinction matters, produce is what a factory does — the work you used to make had a particular texture of aliveness to it, a quality you could feel in the doing of it, and now it doesn't, now it's just output, just the professional performance of a person who still knows how to do the thing without any longer caring about the doing, which is a specific and almost unnameable kind of grief, the grief of losing access to something that is technically still yours, and the cruelest part — the part nobody puts in the LinkedIn post about their sabbatical — is that the burnout doesn't announce itself, doesn't arrive as a dramatic breaking point but as a slow, almost polite withdrawal, a dimming, until one day you realize you can't remember the last time the work felt like anything at all, and you are still, somehow, doing it, and it is fine, and fine is the whole problem.
