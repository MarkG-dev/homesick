There is a specific moment — and if you have been here you will know exactly what I mean, which is part of why it is worth trying to describe precisely rather than gesturing at it from a safe general distance — when you sit down to do the thing you have, for years, told yourself and others that you love, and you feel nothing. Not the productive nothing of a blank page that is also a kind of potential, but a different nothing: flat, slightly nauseating, the creative equivalent of pressing your tongue to a battery and getting no response. This is burnout, or at least the version of it that matters most and gets talked about least honestly, which is not the dramatic collapse but the quiet subtraction — the way the work that used to feel like it was coming from somewhere real starts to feel like a performance of work, a simulation of caring assembled from the outside in. It happens to people who make things for a living, and also to people who make things because making things was the part of their life that felt most like actually living, and the fact that it happens does not mean you are weak or ungrateful or done — though it will, in the worst hours of it, feel like all three simultaneously. What follows is an attempt to look at what actually causes this, how to recognize it before it has taken everything, and what — and here I want to be careful not to promise more than I can deliver — might constitute a genuine way back, as opposed to the fourteen productivity-adjacent suggestions that mostly involve journaling and going outside, which are not wrong exactly but are not quite sufficient either.
