There is a sentence that every corporation learns to write — learns, or is taught, or absorbs the way a building absorbs the smell of whatever burned inside it once — and that sentence begins with the words "we want to address," which is a way of saying we have decided, after considerable internal discussion (the kind of discussion that happens in rooms where the lawyers sit slightly apart from the communications team, each group performing a careful ignorance of what the other group knows), that addressing is now preferable to not addressing, that the silence has become, as they say in those rooms, "untenable," which is itself a word worth pausing over, since "untenable" means literally that the position can no longer be held, that the hands have begun to slip, that what you were gripping — our customers' trust, let's say, or something like trust, or the functional simulacrum of trust that exists between a person and the machine they use to make their coffee every morning — has already started to fall.

So. The SmartBrew Model 7 Series. We made it. We shipped it.

We — and I want you, reading this, to notice that "we" is doing a great deal of work in this sentence, is in fact doing the work of several hundred human beings, engineers and product managers and one specific quality-assurance analyst in our Duluth facility whose report, dated March 4th, used the word "anomalous" in a way that meant something closer to "dangerous" — we have identified a defect in a subset of units that creates a fire risk during the brewing cycle; which means that the object sitting on your counter, the one you filled with water this morning while still partly asleep, the one around which you organized the first twelve minutes of your day, is the thing we are now asking you to stop using.

This is a recall. Not a "voluntary corrective action" — a recall — and the man or woman who first decided that "voluntary" should appear in the phrase "voluntary recall" understood something shrewd and depressing about the grammar of corporate remorse, which is that it must always leave room for the institution to appear to be choosing accountability rather than collapsing under it.

We are choosing it. Or at least we are performing the choosing of it, which is — and here is the part that should make you uncomfortable, that makes us uncomfortable, or that produces in us the exact emotional texture of discomfort without quite arriving at its moral core — not entirely different.

Full refunds. Full replacements. No receipt required. You can begin at smartbrewrecall.com, where a system that wants, in the way that systems want things (which is to say: mechanically, without appetite, but with great consistency), to process you efficiently will ask for your serial number, your purchase date, your address; the system will not ask how you feel about this, presumably because the system has decided — or been decided for — that how you feel is not the relevant variable.

The relevant variable, the thing we keep returning to, is that we shipped you something that could have hurt you, and we are sorry, and that apology — like the sentence it lives inside, like the recall it is attached to, like the coffee you will make tomorrow in some other machine while this one sits in a box waiting to be collected — does not resolve anything, does not close the loop, does not return you to the morning you had before you read this.

And we knew, when we wrote it, that it wouldn't.
