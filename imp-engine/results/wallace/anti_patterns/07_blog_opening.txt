Creative burnout — and let's pause here to anatomize that word "burnout," which borrows the imagery of fuel exhausted, a fire that wanted to keep going but was given nothing left to consume, which is not quite right, because the thing that happens is less dramatic and more like a slow administrative error accumulating across months — creative burnout is what the industry (meaning: the specific humans inside the industry who decided, at some point, that output was the only legible proof of value) has produced in most of us who have spent any sustained time trying to make something. The creep of it, the gradual draining away of whatever it was that made the work feel like yours rather than like a deliverable, the exact emotional texture of reaching for an idea and finding only the memory of having had ideas — this is not a personal failing, or at least it is not only that; it is the predictable output of a system that attributes no psychology to exhaustion and every psychology to productivity. And you, reading this — catching yourself reading this, perhaps, in a tab opened between two other tabs that also promise restoration — you already know what I am about to describe, which is the problem, which is that knowing changes almost nothing. This post will not fix that. But it will try to locate, somewhere inside the mundane machinery of creative depletion, what actually causes it, how to recognize it before it has taken everything, and what it might mean to return to work that still feels like work worth doing — which is a smaller promise than the one you were hoping for, and the only honest one I can make.
