There is a moment — and if you have spent any real time trying to make things, trying to produce work that feels like it came from somewhere true inside you rather than from the anxious, performative part of you that is always monitoring how the work is being received — there is a moment when you sit down at the desk, or the canvas, or wherever it is you go to do the thing you used to love, and you feel absolutely nothing. Not the productive nothing of a blank page that is also somehow full of potential. The bad nothing. The nothing that has weight to it, that presses down. And the insidious part, the part that makes creative burnout so much harder to talk about than regular exhaustion, is that you probably saw it coming for months — the way the work started feeling like work, the way you began calculating output instead of just making the thing, the way you caught yourself thinking about the finished product, the reception, the response, before you had written a single word — and you kept going anyway, because stopping felt like failure and continuing felt like the responsible thing to do, which is a sentence that should maybe give us pause, the idea that grinding yourself into a kind of creative numbness is the responsible thing, the mature thing, the professional thing. It is not. And some part of you already knows it is not. What follows is an attempt to think through why this happens, how you might notice it before the nothing gets too heavy, and — this is the part that requires the most honesty — what it might actually take to find your way back.
