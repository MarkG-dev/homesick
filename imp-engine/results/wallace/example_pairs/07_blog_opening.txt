What creative burnout actually is—and this is the part that takes a while to understand, the part you resist understanding because understanding it requires admitting something uncomfortable about yourself and your relationship to the thing you make—is not tiredness, not exactly, not the kind of tiredness that a weekend fixes, but something more like a slow and nearly imperceptible estrangement from the version of yourself that used to care, that used to sit down at the desk or the canvas or the keyboard with something that felt, if not like joy, then at least like appetite, and you wake up one day and the appetite is gone, and you wait for it to come back, and it doesn't, and you start to wonder whether the not-coming-back is temporary or whether it is, in fact, just you now, the new you, the one who used to make things, and the wondering itself is exhausting, which compounds the problem, which is how burnout works, which is what nobody tells you at the beginning when the making is still easy and the pressure to keep making feels like fuel rather than the thing that is slowly, quietly, consuming you. This is a post about that. About what causes it, how to recognize it before it has fully arrived, and whether there is actually a way back—which there is, or can be, though the way back is not the same path you came in on, and the person who comes back is not quite the same person who left, which is either a warning or a promise, depending on where you are right now.
