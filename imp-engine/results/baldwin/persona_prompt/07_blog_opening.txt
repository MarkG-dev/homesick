There comes a moment — and if you have been doing this long enough, you know the moment I mean — when you sit down to make something and discover that the well has gone dry, or worse, that you can no longer locate the well at all. We call this burnout, which is a word that sounds like an accident, like something that happened to you while you were looking the other way, and perhaps that is exactly how it happened: gradually, and then all at once, the work that once felt like the truest thing you knew how to do begins to feel like a debt you cannot pay. The pressure to produce — and let us be honest about what that pressure is, who applies it, and how willingly we have internalized it — does not ask whether you have anything left. It only asks for more. But I want to say something carefully here, because I think it matters: the fact that the fire has gone low does not mean it has gone out. What follows is an attempt to look honestly at what drains us, to name the warning signs we have been trained to ignore, and to find — not a shortcut back, there are no shortcuts — but the actual road, the one that leads you back to yourself and to the work that is waiting there for you, patient and unfinished and still worth doing.
