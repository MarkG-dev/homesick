Creative burnout is not a mood, not a rough patch, not the ordinary fatigue of a Tuesday — it is the consequence of a system that has taught us to apologize for the limits our bodies and minds actually have, a system we did not build alone but that we have, and I want you to sit with this, helped to maintain, brick by careful brick, every time we pushed through instead of stopping. It arrives slowly; it arrives the way debt arrives, not as a single catastrophic moment but as the accumulated interest on every hour we spent performing productivity instead of practicing it. I have felt it — the morning you open the work and the work opens nothing back, when the thing you made yourself for has become the thing you are hiding from — and I tell you that experience is not private failure, it is public testimony about what we have collectively agreed to demand from creative people, from ourselves. We are not burned out because we are weak; we are burned out because we have been working, furiously and invisibly, to convince ourselves that exhaustion is just the price of ambition. But burnout is not permanent, and this matters: not as comfort, but as instruction, because understanding what built the fire is the only way to stop feeding it. This post intends to name the mechanisms — the warning signs we arrange ourselves not to see, the causes we keep at a convenient distance, the specific and recoverable path back to work that does not cost you the self who does it — and I intend to name them plainly.
