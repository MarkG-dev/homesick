There comes a point — and if you have spent any real time making things, you know this point, you have stood in it like a man standing in a room that has slowly run out of air — when the work you loved becomes a kind of sentence. Not the kind that means anything. Just confinement. The brushes sit untouched. The cursor blinks with the patience of an indifferent god. You find yourself performing the gestures of a creative person while something essential has gone quiet inside you, and the silence is not peaceful but accusatory. We call this burnout, and we have made it sound clinical, manageable, a scheduling problem dressed up in the language of wellness. But I want to suggest that it is something more serious than that — a reckoning with what we were asked to produce, and for whom, and at what cost, and whether anyone ever intended to pay it. What follows is an attempt to look honestly at what drains the life from creative work, how to recognize the signs before you have given everything away, and how — if such a thing is possible, and I believe it is — to find your way back to something true.
