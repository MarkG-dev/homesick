Creative burnout does not announce itself. It arrives the way debt arrives, or the way a marriage ends — gradually, then all at once, and by the time you have a name for what is happening you have already been living inside it for longer than you know. What it takes from you is not just the energy to work but the memory of why you ever wanted to, and that is the cruelest part, that the thing it hollows out first is the very feeling that once made the work feel necessary. The pressure this culture places on people who make things — the relentless, cheerful, merciless demand that you produce and produce and produce, that you be not just creative but consistently, measurably, monetizably creative — is not incidental to the burnout; it is the burnout, dressed up in the language of opportunity. But I want to tell you that this is not a permanent condition, that the people who have lost their way back to the work have, in fact, found it, and that understanding what burned you — really understanding it, not just the symptoms but the source — is the only map that has ever been worth following.
