That condition we have fallen into the habit of naming creative burnout—though I should hesitate over so blunt a term for so insidious a thing—is one that visits, soon or late, very nearly all of us who have staked our living upon the making of things. It does not arrive, I think, by any open assault. It encroaches; and the colour withdraws so gradually from the days that one is some while in perceiving oneself emptied, uninspired, and—of all the estrangements the least bearable—grown a stranger to the very work one had once most loved. In an age so quickened and so insatiable, forever exacting of one the fresh production, the cost of the thing presses, I cannot doubt, upon the mind itself, upon that obscure faculty we are content to call our creativity. And yet the condition is not, I am moved to believe, of necessity a lasting one. In what follows I shall attempt—for I try, here, for the truer terms—to set down what it is that wakes the trouble, by what faint signs it may first be discerned, and through what patient means one might, in the end, be brought the long way back.
