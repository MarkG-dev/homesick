Creative burnout—I give it the name reluctantly, the term being at once too blunt and too pale for the thing it would cover—is, as I now look back upon my own seasons of it, a visitation that comes soon or late to nearly all of us who have staked our living upon the making of things. It does not announce itself; it encroaches. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, the colour withdraws from the days, until one finds oneself emptied, uninspired, and—of the estrangements the cruellest—estranged from the very work one had once most loved. And in an age so quickened and so insatiable, forever exacting the fresh thing, the cost of it falls, I think, upon the mind itself, upon that obscure faculty we are content to call our creativity. Yet the condition, I am glad to reflect, is not a final one. In what follows I shall try to set down what it is that wakes the trouble, by what faint signs it may first be known, and through what patient means one may, at the last, find the long way back.
