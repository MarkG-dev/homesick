Creative burnout—I give it the name reluctantly, the term being at once too blunt and too pale for the thing it would cover—is a visitation that comes, soon or late, to nearly all of us who have staked our living upon the making of things. It does not announce itself. It encroaches: slowly, almost imperceptibly, the colour withdraws from the days, so that the inner place, with its grey sky and its withered garlands, comes to seem like a theatre after the performance, all strewn with crumpled playbills, and one finds oneself emptied, uninspired, estranged—estranged, of the cruelties the cruellest, from the very work one once most loved. In an age so quick, so insatiable, forever exacting the fresh thing, the cost of it falls heavily upon the mind, upon that fine faculty we call our creativity. Yet it is not a final dark. In what follows I shall try—I try for terms!—to set down what wakes the trouble, by what faint signs it is first to be known, and by what patient means one may, at the last, find the long way back.
