Creative burnout—and I find I can hardly utter the term without a wish to qualify it, since it is at once too blunt and too pale for the condition it would name—is a visitation that comes, soon or late, to very nearly all of us who have staked our lives upon the making of things. It arrives, as I now look back and labour for the truer word, not as a blow but as an encroachment, a slow and almost imperceptible withdrawal of the colour from one's days, until one discovers oneself emptied, uninspired, and—cruellest of the estrangements—estranged from the very work one had once most deeply loved. And in an age so quickened, so insatiable, so perpetually exacting of fresh production, the cost of it presses, I think, upon the mind itself, upon that obscure and fragile faculty we are content to call our creativity. Yet the condition, I am glad to suppose, is not a final one. In what follows I shall try to set down what it is that wakes the trouble, by what faint signs it may first be read, and through what patient and unhurried means one may at the last contrive the long way back.
