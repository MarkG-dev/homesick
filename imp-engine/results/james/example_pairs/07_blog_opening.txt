There had come to me, more than once and out of my very devotion to the work, the appalling alarm of the thing they call creative burnout—a visitation that falls, soon or late, upon nearly all of us who have staked our lives upon the making; and it comes, I now reflect, not as a blow but as a slow encroachment, blowing out half one's lights, so that the inner place, with its grey sky and its withered garlands, is like a theatre after the performance, all strewn with crumpled playbills. One finds oneself emptied, uninspired, estranged—estranged, of all the cruelties, from the very work one once most loved. And in an age so quick, so insatiable, so perpetually exacting, the cost of it falls hideously upon the mind, upon that fine faculty we call our creativity. Yet it is not, I am glad to suppose, a final dark. In what follows I shall try—I try for terms!—to set down what wakes the trouble, by what faint signs it is first to be read, and by what patient means one may, at the last, relight the lamps and find the long way back.
