Creative burnout—I name it, as one names the others, with a certain reluctance, since the word is at once too plain and not nearly plain enough—is a thing that visits, sooner or later, very nearly all of us who live by the making of things. It comes, as I now look back and try for terms, not with a blow but by a slow encroachment, an almost imperceptible draining of the colour from the days, until one finds oneself emptied, uninspired, estranged—estranged, of all the cruelties, from the very work one had once most loved. And in this age of ours, so quick, so insatiable, so perpetually demanding of fresh production, the cost of it falls heavily upon the mind, upon that fragile, that hardly-to-be-named faculty we call our creativity. Yet the condition is not, I am glad to suppose, a final one. In what follows I shall try to set down something of what wakes the trouble, how its first faint signs may be read, and by what patient means one may find, at the last, the long way back.
